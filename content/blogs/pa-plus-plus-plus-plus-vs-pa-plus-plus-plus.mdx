Most people reading a sunscreen label look at the SPF number and stop there. The PA rating sitting next to it is easy to overlook, but it measures something SPF does not: protection against UVA radiation, the wavelength most responsible for premature ageing and pigmentation.

## What PA Actually Measures

PA stands for Protection Grade of UVA, a rating system developed in Japan and now used across Asia, including India. It is derived from a measurement called **PPD**, or Persistent Pigment Darkening — a lab test that exposes skin to UVA light and records how much longer, relative to unprotected skin, it takes for visible darkening to appear.

The PA scale converts PPD ranges into a simple symbol system, from PA+ to PA++++.

| Rating | PPD Range | Protection Level |
|---|---|---|
| PA+ | 2–4 | Some UVA protection |
| PA++ | 4–8 | Moderate UVA protection |
| PA+++ | 8–16 | High UVA protection |
| PA++++ | 16 and above | Very high UVA protection |

SPF, by contrast, measures protection against UVB, the wavelength responsible for sunburn. A high SPF number says nothing about how well a product handles UVA. Two sunscreens can both carry SPF 50 while one offers PA+++ and the other PA++++, and that difference is meaningful.

## Why This Matters More in India

UVA makes up roughly 95 percent of the UV radiation that reaches the earth's surface, and unlike UVB, it does not vary much by season, cloud cover, or time of day. It penetrates glass. It is present in overcast weather. This is why dermatologists increasingly describe UVA exposure as a year-round condition rather than a summer one.

India sits largely within tropical and subtropical latitudes, meaning UVA exposure is consistently high across most of the country for most of the year. Cities from Delhi to Chennai to Mumbai register elevated UV index readings well outside the conventional "summer season," and UVA's ability to penetrate cloud cover means even monsoon months do not offer meaningful relief.

## The Hyperpigmentation Mechanism

UVA penetrates deeper into the skin than UVB, reaching the dermis where it interacts with melanocytes, the cells responsible for producing melanin. This triggers **oxidative stress** and stimulates melanin production as a protective response. Over repeated exposure, this process is understood to contribute to hyperpigmentation, uneven skin tone, and the breakdown of collagen and elastin that leads to premature signs of ageing.

This is particularly relevant for **Fitzpatrick skin types IV through VI**, which describe medium to deep skin tones and cover the majority of the Indian population. Skin in this range already produces more melanin at baseline and tends to show a stronger pigmentation response to UVA exposure. Studies in dermatology consistently note that darker skin tones are more prone to post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation, whether from acne, injury, or unprotected sun exposure, which means the marginal gain from PA++++ over PA+++ is not cosmetic trivia — it addresses a documented vulnerability.

## PA+++ vs PA++++ in Daily Practice

The difference between PA+++ and PA++++ comes down to the ceiling of protection, not just the floor. PA+++ covers a PPD range of 8 to 16. PA++++ covers 16 and above, with no defined upper limit under the current rating system. For someone who is outdoors briefly, commuting in a car, or working near a window, this gap may seem academic. For someone with regular, cumulative daily UVA exposure — which describes most working adults in Indian cities — the higher ceiling translates into a meaningfully longer window before UVA-driven pigment changes begin to register.

Because PA++++ formulations are held to a stricter UVA-filtering standard, they are also more likely to use broader-spectrum, photostable filter systems rather than the minimum combination needed to clear a lower threshold.

## What to Look For

When comparing sunscreens, the SPF number should not be read in isolation. A PA++++ rating alongside a solid SPF number indicates a more complete defense against the two distinct radiation types that drive different forms of skin damage — sunburn on one side, deep pigmentation and photoageing on the other.
