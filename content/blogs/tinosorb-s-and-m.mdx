If you have used a European sunscreen brand and noticed it felt different from most products sold in the US — less greasy, less prone to that stinging sensation in the eyes, more stable over the course of a day — there is a good chance the reason is one of two filter compounds: Tinosorb S or Tinosorb M.

## What Tinosorb S and M Actually Are

**Tinosorb S**, chemically known as bemotrizinol, and **Tinosorb M**, known as bisoctrizole, are broad-spectrum UV filters developed by BASF. Both are engineered to absorb across a wide range of UVA and UVB wavelengths, which is what allows them to function as near-complete filters rather than needing to be paired with several narrower-spectrum filters to achieve full-spectrum coverage.

Bemotrizinol (Tinosorb S) is an organic, oil-soluble filter typically used in chemical sunscreen formulations. Bisoctrizole (Tinosorb M) is formulated as a micro-fine particle dispersion, which gives it characteristics that sit between a traditional chemical filter and a physical filter — it absorbs and scatters UV radiation, rather than relying on absorption alone.

## The Photostability Problem They Solve

Before filters like these became widely available, one of the persistent challenges in sunscreen formulation was **photostability** — the tendency of some UV filters to break down chemically after exposure to sunlight, gradually losing their protective capacity over the course of a day.

**Avobenzone**, one of the most common UVA filters in older and many current formulations, is known for exactly this weakness. It degrades relatively quickly under UV exposure unless it is stabilized by other ingredients in the formula, and even then, its protection can decline meaningfully within a couple of hours of sun exposure.

Tinosorb S and M are both significantly more photostable. They do not require the same degree of stabilizing support, and they maintain their protective capacity for longer under continuous UV exposure. This is one of the primary reasons formulators favor them in daily-wear sunscreens intended to hold up through a full day rather than requiring frequent reapplication to stay effective.

## Why They Are Called "European Filters"

Tinosorb S and M are approved for use in the European Union and widely used across Asian markets, but neither has been approved by the US FDA for use in sunscreen products. The FDA's sunscreen filter approval process operates under a different regulatory framework than the EU's, and a number of modern, well-studied UV filters — including both Tinosorb compounds — have not gone through that specific approval pathway in the US, regardless of their approval status elsewhere.

This regulatory gap is why these filters are often informally referred to as "European filters," even though their use extends well beyond Europe into many Asian formulations, India included.

## Which Brands Use Them

Tinosorb S and M appear in formulations from several of the most recognized names in dermatologically positioned skincare, including **Bioderma** and **La Roche-Posay**, both of which have built parts of their sun-care lines around these filters specifically for their stability and broad-spectrum coverage. Their presence in a formulation is generally taken as a signal of a more considered, dermatology-oriented approach to UV filtering rather than a minimum-compliance formulation.

## Price and Value Comparison

Sunscreens built around Tinosorb S and M from international brands typically carry a premium price in the Indian market, often ranging from ₹800 to over ₹1,500 per tube, reflecting import costs and brand positioning as much as the raw cost of the filter technology itself.

Therazo's Daily Shield uses this same filter technology at a domestic price point, aiming to make photostable, broad-spectrum protection accessible without the cost structure that comes with importing an equivalent international formulation. The filter chemistry inside the tube is the same category of technology found in significantly higher-priced products — the difference is in how it is priced and distributed, not in the underlying science.
