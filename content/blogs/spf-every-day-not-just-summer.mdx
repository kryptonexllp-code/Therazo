Ask most people in India when they wear sunscreen, and the honest answer is usually "in summer, if I remember." This pattern is understandable — it mirrors how sunscreen has traditionally been marketed, as a hot-weather product for beach trips and afternoon commutes. It does not, however, reflect how UV radiation actually behaves.

## UV Index Data Does Not Support a Seasonal Approach

The UV Index, a standard measure of ground-level UV radiation intensity, remains high across most Indian cities for the majority of the year. India's geography places most major population centers within tropical and subtropical latitudes, where the sun's angle stays high enough to produce significant UV exposure well outside the conventional summer months.

Cities like Chennai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad, and Mumbai routinely register UV index readings in the "high" to "very high" range across seasons that would not typically be associated with sun protection — including the monsoon and winter months many people associate with lower sun risk.

## Cloud Cover and Winter Do Not Mean Safety

This is where the seasonal mental model breaks down. **UVB**, the radiation responsible for sunburn, is reduced meaningfully by cloud cover and lower winter sun angles. **UVA**, however, behaves very differently. It penetrates cloud cover with far less attenuation and remains largely consistent in intensity across seasons.

Because UVA is the wavelength most linked to deep skin penetration, oxidative stress, and long-term photoageing, a cloudy or cool day offers a false sense of security. Skin can be accumulating UVA-driven damage even when there is no sunburn risk and no obvious visual cue that protection is needed.

## Photoageing Works Differently on Darker Skin Tones

For the Fitzpatrick skin types most common in the Indian population — generally types IV through VI — the visible signs of UV damage often present differently than they do in lighter skin. Rather than early wrinkling, cumulative UV exposure in darker skin tones is more commonly associated with **hyperpigmentation, uneven tone, and dullness**, changes that build gradually and are easy to attribute to other causes like diet, sleep, or pollution.

Because the damage is cumulative and the visible signal is subtle, the absence of a dramatic sunburn can create a false impression that protection was not needed. In reality, the daily, low-grade UVA exposure that darker skin experiences year-round in India is a well-documented driver of exactly these pigmentation changes.

## The Compliance Gap

Dermatological surveys and clinical observation in India consistently point to a **sunscreen compliance gap** — a large share of the population owns sunscreen but does not apply it consistently, and an even larger share does not apply it at all outside of the hottest months. This gap is not primarily a product availability problem. It is a habit and mental-model problem: sunscreen is still framed as a summer item rather than a daily one.

## Reframing SPF as a Daily Habit

The more useful framework, and the one most dermatologists now recommend, is to treat SPF the way one treats brushing teeth — a fixed part of the morning routine, independent of weather, season, or how sunny it looks outside. This does not require a different product for each season. It requires consistency.

A broad-spectrum sunscreen applied every morning, rain or shine, addresses the exposure that actually accumulates over a year — not just the exposure that is visible or uncomfortable in the moment. For Indian skin, where UV exposure is a year-round condition rather than a seasonal event, that daily consistency is the single highest-leverage habit change available.
